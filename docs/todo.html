    How the page is bound to the program
    


    The program says Use the page library. and from then on thirteen kinds of thing are elements of the page. This page uses seven of them: a panel, a heading, a note, a field, a button, a card and a gauge. The others are pictures, links, tables and menus, which the page library's own page describes. Making one puts it on the page, in the order made. Nothing else has to be called. Use the list above, and watch the things on the right change with it.

    Nothing on the page is brought up to date by hand. The chapters say what each thing is always to be, in sentences beginning Keep: which cards are hidden, what order they stand in, what the counts and the note say. After every change the language works those out again. Then, however much one click changed, the list is saved once, by the sentence Whenever everything has settled:.
    


    An earlier version did all of this in a verb called Tidy, run after every single change. Pressing “Tick them all” on two hundred cards took thirteen seconds and saved the list two hundred and one times. It now takes nine thousandths of a second and saves it once. The measurements are written up.
    


    The binding goes both ways and is kept by the library, which is a few hundred lines of Python. After every sentence that a click sets off, it compares the things with the elements and changes only what differs, so the box you are typing in is never rebuilt under you. The sentences beginning Whenever are part of the language, not of the library: the grammar describes them, and any program may use them.
    


    Why it is a book
    


    The program is five files. Four of them are chapters of a book called tasks, which is a folder, and the fifth is the program itself, which says Use the layout chapter of the tasks book. and so on. Each chapter does one job and says, in its first sentence, which of its words the other files may use.
    


    
  
    ChapterWhat it does, and what it shares
    
  
    
    layoutMakes what is on the page. It shares the things the others change by name, such as Entry and Tally, and keeps the panels that only hold things in place to itself.
    
    
    cardsSays what a task is, and keeps each card in its place in the list. It shares the adjective open and the verb jot.
    
    
    keepingRemembers the list between visits, and the last card removed. How a card is written as a line of text is known only here.
    
    
    showingKeeps the right cards in sight and the counts true. Which view is in force is its own business: no other file can read it or change it, except by asking it to favour a view. It shares that one verb and nothing else.
    
      

    


    What is left in the program is a short list of sentences beginning Whenever, which is the part worth reading first. A chapter that tried to use a word another chapter keeps to itself would be refused, with a message naming the chapter that keeps it. Section 9.6 of the grammar gives the rules.
    


    As everywhere on this site, the compiler here is the real one, running as WebAssembly: the machinery explains how. You can change any of the five files above and run the program again. Try making the heading say something else in layout, or making finished cards stand first in cards.
